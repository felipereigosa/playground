## Claude

Short answer: you can't do it with a plain WebView. There are three real options, and I'd pick the third.

(I took "moonlight" and "moonshot" to mean the same thing: your Moonlight XR fork at `~/Files/projects/virtual-desktop` on the laptop.)

**Why a WebView won't work:** Android's WebView has never supported WebXR. `navigator.xr` either isn't there or can't start an `immersive-vr` session. Meta's Spatial SDK web panels are flat 2D too. On the Quest, immersive WebXR runs in a full browser engine that owns an OpenXR session: Quest Browser, or Wolvic (built on GeckoView).

**The options:**

1. **A button in Moonlight XR that opens Quest Browser at the playground URL.** This is an Android intent with package `com.oculus.browser`, about 10 lines of code. It's still an app switch, but one click and no hunting for the URL. You'd still press "Enter VR" in the page, because the browser wants a user gesture for that, and as far as I know being launched by an intent doesn't count. Going back the other way with an `intent://` link may or may not work in Quest Browser; I haven't checked.

2. **Embed GeckoView in Moonlight XR, the way Wolvic does.** GeckoView can run WebXR, but only if the host app supplies the VR side: Wolvic has a lot of native code that shares its OpenXR session and frames with Gecko. Moonlight XR already owns an OpenXR session, so this is possible in principle. In practice it's weeks of work and a library of roughly 100 MB, and you'd be maintaining a browser integration. I wouldn't do it.

3. **Flip it around and bring the desktop into the playground.** You'd stay in Quest Browser all the time, and the desktop becomes a gadget you summon, which matches the "personal VR universe" idea. The laptop's screen would come in over WebRTC and be drawn as a video texture on a three.js plane. There are two ways to send it:
   - A small page on the laptop calls `getDisplayMedia` and sends the screen to the playground over WebRTC. The signalling can go through the dev server you already run with `npm start`. The Quest Browser decodes the video in hardware, so it's fine for desktop work, but latency won't match Moonlight's for games.
   - Use Sunshine itself, through a browser-side Moonlight client. I believe there's an open-source project, `moonlight-web-stream`, that bridges Sunshine to WebRTC. That would give you Sunshine's encoder and mouse and keyboard handling inside WebXR. I haven't checked how mature it is.

   You'd lose what's special about Moonlight XR: the depth-based 3D, the rooms and the tuned frame pacing. That only matters if you want those for games. For working inside the playground, a flat desktop panel is enough, and you could keep Moonlight XR for gaming.

My recommendation: do option 1 now because it's cheap, and make option 3 the long-term plan, since it removes the app switch for good and fits where the playground is heading. If you want, I can start option 3 with a desktop gadget that uses `getDisplayMedia` and test it in the emulator first.

I've seen `resources/hands.js`, an old three.js hand-tracking example. As far as I can tell the playground itself (`src/`) only uses controllers right now. `page.hand` in the emulator tests moves a controller, not a tracked hand. So hands would be new work, though not a big step. Here's my take on each part.

**How accurate is WebXR hand tracking?**
On Quest, WebXR gets the same hand tracking as native apps: 25 joints per hand, each with a position, a rotation and a radius, predicted to the display rate. When your hand is in front of you and the cameras can see it, accuracy is good, roughly within a centimetre. It gets worse in a few predictable cases:
- Fingers hidden behind other fingers, or behind the back of the hand.
- Hands low, at your sides or behind you, out of the cameras' view. You can't rest your hands in your lap while you work.
- Fast movement, and one hand over the other.

One more catch: the tracker fits a hand model with built-in assumptions about hand shapes. When it can't see a finger, it guesses a plausible pose instead of reporting nothing. So a near-touch can show up as a touch.

**Buttons as thumb-to-fingertip touches**
- **Thumb to index:** very reliable. It's the gesture Meta tuned the whole system around.
- **Thumb to middle:** decent.
- **Thumb to ring and pinky:** noticeably worse. The thumb crosses behind the other fingers and the tracker mixes up ring and pinky. I'd treat these as rare actions, or skip them.

Ways to make touches dependable:
- Two thresholds, so a press doesn't flicker on and off. For example, a touch starts under about 1.5 cm and only ends past 3 cm.
- Scale those distances to the size of your hand.
- Briefly confirm the touch before acting on it.
- A click sound and a glow on the fingertip. With no haptics, feedback is what makes it feel solid.

One conflict to know about: a pinch with your palm facing your face opens the Quest system menu. Our gestures should avoid that pose.

**Navigation**
- **Fist as a joystick, rotation version:** this is the stronger of your two ideas. Wrist orientation is the most robust thing the tracker gives us. Capture a reference orientation the moment you make the fist, then treat tilt away from it as stick deflection, with a dead zone. It doesn't matter where your hand is when you start.
- **Fist as a joystick, position version:** make a fist and move your hand away from where it started. Position tracks even better than rotation, and it feels like grabbing a lever.
- **Thumb over a fist as a thumbstick:** weaker. When your fist faces away from you, your hand hides the thumb from the cameras, and the movements are only millimetres.
- **Meta's own approach:** Meta solved part of this natively with "microgestures": thumb swipes along the side of the index finger (left, right, forward, back, tap), used for snap turning and teleporting. WebXR doesn't expose those, but we could build a rough version from the joint data.
- **Grab the world and pull yourself:** worth trying for the playground especially. Fist on one hand and drag to move. Fists on both hands, then rotate or pull apart to turn or scale the world. It suits a personal universe full of gadgets better than a thumbstick does, and it looks great with hands.

**A custom hand model and the watch**
Yes to both.
- **Your own hand model:** WebXR gives us joint poses, and we can drive any mesh with them. One way is a rigged glTF with bones named after the WebXR joints. Three.js's hand mesh code accepts your own model if the names match. Another way is a hand built from shapes in code. The hand could also be built in Blender, the same way as the watch.
- **The watch:** today it's exported in the left grip's space. For hands we'd attach it to the `wrist` joint instead, with a new offset that we'd measure. The joint sits at the base of the palm, so the watch goes a little behind it, on the forearm.
- **Opening the menu:** the fingertip touch that opens it already works the same with hands. Touch the watch with your other index finger.
- **Hands and controllers together:** Quest switches between hands and controllers automatically when you pick them up or put them down. So the watch and menu can follow whichever input you're using, without having to choose one for good.

**What I'd prototype first:** your hand model with the watch, a thumb-to-index pinch for grabbing gadgets, and grab-the-world for moving around. Then add thumb-to-middle as a second button and try the fist joystick against grab-the-world. One problem: the emulator tests drive controllers, and IWER's hand support is limited, so most of the tuning would have to happen on the Quest.

**How it decides**
- **Clenched:** all four fingertips are close to the wrist. The thumb doesn't count, so a fist with the thumb out is still a fist.
- **Pointing:** the index finger is straight and the other three are curled.
- The test measures how far each fingertip is from the wrist, counted in palm lengths. An open hand measures about 1.9 and a fist about 0.7.
- A finger counts as curled below 1.3 and only as straight again above 1.6. That gap stops a hand on the edge from flickering between states.
